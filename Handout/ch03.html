Objective 3.1

What counts as ethical?

Before a company can claim to be ethical, we need a shared definition of the word.

Ethics
The study of the general nature of morals and the specific moral principles that govern a person's behavior.

Two systems for deciding what's right

Not everyone reasons about right and wrong the same way. Tap each card to see how it works.

Moral relativism
tap to flip
There is no universal moral truth — only individual beliefs, perspectives, and values. What's "right" depends on who you ask.
Situational ethics
tap to flip
Decisions are made based on the specific situation at hand, instead of applying one universal law to every case.

Unethical vs. amoral — not the same thing

Unethical behavior
Behavior that does not conform to a set of approved standards of social or professional behavior.
Amoral behavior
Behavior from a person who has no sense of right and wrong, and no interest in the moral consequences of their actions — a step beyond simply being unethical.

Your own code

Personal ethics
Principles that guide the decisions you make in your life.

Three ethical frameworks

When people try to justify a decision, they're usually leaning on one of these three approaches — often without realizing it.

Utilitarianism
consequence-based · tap to flip
Judges an action by its outcomes: the right choice produces the greatest good for the greatest number of people.
Deontology
duty-based · tap to flip
Judges an action by whether it follows a moral rule or duty — regardless of the outcome it produces.
Virtue ethics
character-based · tap to flip
Judges an action by whether it reflects good character traits — honesty, courage, fairness — rather than rules or outcomes.
Objective 3.2

Personal ethics meets business ethics

You don't leave your personal ethics at the door when you clock in.

  • Our personal ideas of right and wrong influence our actions, words, and thoughts — on the job and off.
  • Off-the-job behavior, integrity, and honesty relate directly to on-the-job performance.

How do you read a company's ethics?

Code of ethics (code of conduct)
A company's written statement of its commitment to certain ethical practices — one of the clearest windows into how an organization expects its people to behave.

Corporate social responsibility

Corporate social responsibility (CSR)
A company's obligation to conduct its activities with the aim of achieving social, environmental, and economic development.

Benefits, measurement, challenges, effects

The benefits of CSR▼
  • A positive reputation in the marketplace with consumers, suppliers, and vendors
  • Stronger recruitment and retention of talent
  • Efficiency gains when materials are used resourcefully and waste is minimized
  • Sales growth through new product innovation and ethically conscious labeling
How to measure CSR▼
  • Social audits — a study of how well a company meets its social responsibilities
  • Ratings and rankings from outside organizations
  • Self-reporting, which is becoming more common
  • Corporate philanthropy — donating profits or resources to charitable causes
The challenges of CSR▼
  • Balancing investor returns against the desire to reduce human suffering is genuinely hard
  • Some companies manage to balance both consistently — but it takes deliberate effort
The effects of CSR▼
  • Environmental effects, both local and global
  • Economic effects on prices, availability, and product quality
  • Effects on employee morale, shaped by a company's ethical culture
  • Effects on social justice — equal treatment, equal pay, and protection from discrimination

Objective 3.4

Legal compliance and ethical conduct

Being legal and being ethical are related — but they are not the same thing.

Legal regulations
The specific laws governing the products or processes of an industry.
Legal compliance
Conducting a business within the boundaries of all the legal regulations of that industry.

Objective 3.5

Ethics as a business opportunity

Doing the right thing and building a profitable business aren't always in tension — sometimes the ethical gap is the market opportunity.

Creating new markets with an ethical focus

☀
Green energy
Companies don't need to invent new solar technology — there's a market in simply making clean energy affordable to switch to.
⚕
Developing new medicines
Orphan diseases are rare and traditionally unprofitable to treat — but persistence has turned some of them into viable markets.
♻
Reclaimed or recycled materials
Ocean plastic and other reclaimed waste have become source material for an entirely new category of products.

Going green

Sustainability
The process of improving the quality of people's lives in ways that simultaneously protect and enhance the earth's life support systems.
Objective 3.6

Building an ethical environment

Getting employees off to an ethical start

How can a business improve its own culture of ethical and responsible conduct?

  • Keep a strict, current code of ethics and mission statement in place — and actually communicate it to employees.
  • Offer orientation programs so new employees know the ethical standards from day one.

Keeping it alive every day

How can a business maintain its policy of ethical conduct in the workplace?

  • Managers talk regularly with employees about acceptable and unacceptable practices.
  • Anonymous hotlines let employees report violations without fear.
  • Ongoing ethics training programs keep awareness from fading over time.
Special focus

Shareholder theory vs. stakeholder theory

Two of the most influential — and opposing — answers to a single question: who is a business actually responsible to?

Shareholder theory

Milton Friedman · 1970

The task of a business is to use its resources and engage in activities designed to increase its profits — as long as it stays within the rules of the game: open, free competition, without deception or fraud.

"There is one and only one social responsibility of business — to use its resources and engage in activities designed to increase its profits so long as it stays within the rules of the game."
Stakeholder theory

R. Edward Freeman · 1984

The task of executives is to create as much value as possible for stakeholders without resorting to tradeoffs. Businesses exist to do more than just make profits and money for shareholders.

Ignoring stakeholders can harm a company's reputation and its long-term success — even if it helps this quarter's numbers.
